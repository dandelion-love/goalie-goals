# Summary

A training teen needs enough energy for growth plus training, spread across the day from
ordinary foods, with water as the usual drink. International and dietitian bodies agree
that supplements, energy drinks and weight goals have no place in a youth programme the
app runs. Detailed diet advice belongs to a registered dietitian or doctor.

# Energy and eating pattern

- Exact energy needs of youth athletes are hard to pin down; carbohydrate and fat intake
  should follow established guidelines, and protein needs can be met by well-timed eating
  without protein supplements.[^ioc-2015]
- Sports dietitians recommend eating for long-term health, matching intake to the day's
  training, and spreading good-quality carbohydrate and protein across the day,
  especially soon after training.[^sda-2014]
- Low energy availability (too little food for the training done) harms health and
  performance in both female and male athletes. The IOC calls this Relative Energy
  Deficiency in Sport (REDs).[^reds-2023]
- Disordered eating is more common in competitive youth sport than in non-athletes, and
  its onset often coincides with specialisation and rising competition.[^ioc-2015]

# Key nutrients

- Calcium, vitamin D and iron deserve attention in adolescent athletes because of an
  elevated risk of shortfall.[^sda-2014][^ioc-2015]
- The IOC notes a calcium recommendation of 1,300 mg a day for adolescents, and that iron
  supplements should be taken only when medically warranted.[^ioc-2015]
- Vitamin D correction "may be necessary" in some youth athletes; that is a decision for
  a health professional after assessment, not for the app.[^ioc-2015]

# Hydration

- Adolescent athletes should have clean, cool fluids available in enough quantity before,
  during and after sport.[^sda-2014] Fluids also support recovery.[^aap-2020-parents]
- For routine activity, water is the right drink; sports drinks are for prolonged,
  vigorous exercise or extreme heat, and frequent sugary sports drinks add unneeded
  energy and dental risk.[^cps-drinks][^aap-2011-drinks]

# Caffeine, energy drinks and supplements: where sources differ

| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| AAP (United States) | Caffeine and other stimulants in energy drinks have no place in the diet of children and adolescents[^aap-2011-drinks] |
| Canadian Paediatric Society | Energy drinks carry caffeine above recommended limits and real risks for youth[^cps-drinks] |
| Health Canada | A precautionary daily caffeine ceiling of 2.5 mg per kg of body weight for adolescents[^hc-caffeine] |
| EFSA (European Union) | Judged up to about 3 mg per kg of body weight a day to be of no safety concern for children and adolescents[^efsa-caffeine] |

- Sports dietitians: nutrient needs should come from food, not supplements; recommending
  supplements to developing athletes oversells them.[^sda-2014]
- The IOC: encouraging performance supplements in youth athletes is inappropriate, and
  supplements carry anti-doping risk.[^ioc-2015]
- **Conservative choice:** the limits differ, but the paediatric bodies say none. The app
  never suggests caffeine, energy drinks or any supplement, and never sets weight-loss,
  weight-gain or body-composition goals.
