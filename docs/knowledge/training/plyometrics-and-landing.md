# Summary

Jump training improves jumping, sprinting and change of direction in youth and is safe when
the dose matches the athlete's ability and safety rules are built in. Landing quality, not
jump height, decides whether a jump exercise can progress.

# Effect

- A systematic review of plyometric programmes in youth found large effects on running and
  jumping, with preliminary evidence for kicking distance, balance and agility. The
  included studies were of low methodological quality.[^johnson-2011]
- A meta-analysis of male youth athletes found plyometric training moderately effective
  for countermovement jump height across maturity groups.[^moran-2017-cmj]
- Plyometric training also improved change-of-direction ability in youth, with slightly
  larger gains in older (mid and post growth spurt) groups.[^asadi-2017]
- Combining plyometric and resistance training appears to give larger benefits than
  either alone, and the NSCA rejects the idea that youth need a set strength standard
  before any age-appropriate jumping.[^nsca-2009]
- Simple hops and jumps are among the activities the AAP names for building strength in
  young people.[^aap-2020-parents]

# Dose: where sources differ

| Source | Suggested dose |
|---|---|
| Johnson 2011 | Twice a week for 8 to 10 weeks, starting at 50 to 60 jumps a session and increasing load weekly; a lower-intensity, longer programme for those who cannot tolerate this[^johnson-2011] |
| Asadi 2017 | Two sessions a week, about 1,400 moderate-intensity jumps over 7 weeks (roughly 100 a session) for change-of-direction gains[^asadi-2017] |

**Conservative choice:** start at no more than 50 to 60 foot contacts a session, twice a
week on non-consecutive days, and never exceed about 100 contacts a session, the average
dose in the higher source. Progress only when every landing in the previous sessions was
controlled.

# Growth stage

- Jump gains were smaller in the 13 to 16 age band (around the growth spurt) than before
  or after it, despite more training. The authors suggest adjusting volume and type of
  training during this window.[^moran-2017-cmj]
- Growth-related injuries at the knee and heel tendon attachments are a known risk during
  rapid growth.[^ioc-2015]
- **App implication:** during reported fast growth, keep jump volume at or below the
  starting dose and favour low-impact variants.

# Landing mechanics and safety

- Injury becomes possible when the intensity, volume or frequency of plyometric work
  exceeds the participant's abilities.[^nsca-2009]
- Plyometric training was judged safe in the review when consent, the child's agreement
  and built-in safety guidelines were all present.[^johnson-2011]
- Power exercises should be done in few, high-quality repetitions so speed and technique
  hold for every repetition.[^nsca-2009]
- Practical safety rules the app enforces (drawn from the sources above as a cautious
  reading, not separately tested):
  - jumps on a forgiving surface (grass, a mat or a sprung floor), never on concrete;
  - land softly through the balls of the feet, knees tracking over toes, hips back, and
    hold the landing before the next jump;
  - teach landings ("stick it") before any repeated or reactive jumps;
  - no depth or drop jumps from a height, and no added weight on jumps;
  - stop the set when landings get loud, knees cave in, or form breaks.
