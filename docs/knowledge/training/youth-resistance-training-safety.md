# Summary

Resistance training is safe and useful for an early-teens athlete when it is well designed,
supervised, and technique comes before load. The main injury risks in the literature come
from missing supervision, poor technique and loads that are too heavy, not from strength
training as such.

# Is it safe at this age?

- Leading strength and paediatric bodies agree that a properly designed and supervised
  programme is relatively safe for youth and can improve strength, power, motor skill,
  sports performance and resistance to sports injury.[^nsca-2009][^lloyd-2014][^aap-2020]
- The AAP sets no minimum age; readiness depends on training experience ("training age")
  as much as on calendar age.[^aap-2020-parents]
- The World Health Organization asks adolescents to include muscle- and bone-strengthening
  activity on at least three days a week as part of daily activity.[^who-2020]
- The NSCA review traces reported injuries mostly to unsupervised settings, improper
  technique, lifting maximal loads, or no qualified supervisor.[^nsca-2009]
- Every youth is an athlete in need of individual, long-term development, not an adult
  programme scaled down.[^nsca-ltad-2016]

# Supervision

- Qualified instruction and supervision is the first item of the NSCA's general
  guidelines, together with a safe, hazard-free training area.[^nsca-2009]
- The 2014 International Consensus (adapted from the UK Strength and Conditioning
  Association statement and endorsed by paediatric and sports-medicine bodies) likewise
  makes qualified supervision and instruction a condition of safe youth
  training.[^lloyd-2014]
- For a home setting the app cannot supply a qualified coach. The parent is the
  supervisor, so the app must keep to exercises a non-specialist can watch and correct
  (see [training rules](/decisions/training-rules.md)).

# Technique first, then load

The NSCA general guidelines, paraphrased:[^nsca-2009]

| Guideline | Detail |
|---|---|
| Warm-up | 5 to 10 minutes of dynamic warm-up before each session |
| Start light | Begin with relatively light loads and always focus on correct technique |
| Beginners | 1 to 2 sets of 10 to 15 repetitions with light to moderate load while learning technique |
| Strength work | 1 to 3 sets of 6 to 15 repetitions across upper- and lower-body exercises |
| Power work | 1 to 3 sets of 3 to 6 repetitions, with few, high-quality repetitions |
| Progression | Increase resistance gradually, by 5 to 10%, as strength improves |
| Frequency | Begin with 2 to 3 sessions a week on non-consecutive days |
| Cool-down | Lower-intensity calisthenics and stretching |
| Listen | Attend to each athlete's needs and concerns during the session; keep a training log |

- A dynamic warm-up improved jump performance compared with static stretching in
  adolescent athletes, which supports dynamic warm-ups before power work.[^faigenbaum-2006]
- The AAP adds: train all major muscle groups, combine resistance training with aerobic and
  skill work, warm up dynamically, and cool down with lighter stretching.[^aap-2020-parents]
- Bodyweight, elastic bands and medicine balls are all accepted training modes; simple
  exercises should come first, progressing to multi-joint movements as competence
  grows.[^nsca-2009]

# Maximal lifts: where sources disagree

- The NSCA and the 2014 International Consensus report that one-repetition-maximum (1RM)
  testing has been done safely in youth, but only with adequate warm-up, gradual loading
  and close supervision by experienced professionals.[^nsca-2009][^lloyd-2014]
- The older AAP policy was more cautious about maximal lifting and competitive lifting while
  the skeleton is still maturing.[^aap-2008]
- **Conservative choice:** a home setting has no qualified professional, so the app never
  prescribes or tests maximal or near-maximal lifts. Effort is judged by clean repetitions,
  not by finding a maximum.

# Growth and maturation

- Puberty and the adolescent growth spurt fall roughly between ages 9 and 15, and athletes
  of the same age can differ widely in biological maturity.[^ioc-2015]
- Rapid growth and maturation can predispose youth to specific injuries of the immature
  spine, joint surfaces and growth-plate attachments (traction apophysitis, for example at
  the knee below the kneecap or at the heel). Injuries to growth centres are a particular
  concern.[^ioc-2015]
- Development should be judged individually against each athlete's own growth, not
  against age group norms.[^ioc-2015]
- Responses to plyometric training are smaller around the growth spurt than before or
  after it (see [plyometrics](/training/plyometrics-and-landing.md)).
- **App implication:** when the parent reports a period of fast growth, the app holds
  progressions and lowers jump and sprint volume rather than adding load.
