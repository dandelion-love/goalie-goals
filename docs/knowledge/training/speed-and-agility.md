# Summary

Short sprints with full recovery, and jump-based change-of-direction work, improve speed
and agility in youth. Goalkeepers need short, explosive movement over a few metres more
than long sprints.

# Sprint training

- In a meta-analysis of male youth athletes, sprint training was moderately effective
  overall, with larger effects in athletes around and after the growth spurt than before
  it.[^moran-2017-sprint]
- The same authors suggest about two sessions a week of up to 16 sprints of around 20 m
  (about 22 yd), with long rests (a work-to-rest ratio of 1:25, or more than 90
  seconds).[^moran-2017-sprint]
- Training choices should follow biological maturity, not just age.[^moran-2017-sprint]
  [^nsca-ltad-2016]
- Resisted sprinting (sled towing at 2.5 to 10% of body mass) improved sprint performance
  in mid- and post-growth-spurt youth but not in younger ones.[^rumpf-2015] It needs
  equipment and coaching that a home setting usually lacks, so the app leaves it out.

# Agility and change of direction

- Plyometric training improves change-of-direction ability in youth; two sessions a week
  were enough for meaningful gains.[^asadi-2017]
- Agility work for goalkeepers should therefore come mostly from short shuffles, crossover
  steps, and jumps into a set position, done at full quality with full recovery.

# Goalkeeper context

- Goalkeepers spend most of a match at low intensity and cover roughly half the distance
  of outfield players; the decisive moments are a handful of explosive saves.[^white-2018]
- Studies on goalkeeper speed and agility are mixed: some show values similar to outfield
  players, others lower.[^ziv-2011] Goalkeepers tend to be slower over longer sprints but
  better jumpers.[^white-2018]
- **App implication:** favour 2 to 10 m (about 2 to 11 yd) reactive starts, shuffles and
  dives over long sprints, and count sprint efforts toward the same weekly limit as jumps.
