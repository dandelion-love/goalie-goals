# Summary

For an early-teens athlete, the honest promise of a strength or trunk exercise is better
control and more strength, mostly from the nervous system learning to use the muscles it
already has. Gains are real, arrive as ranges over weeks to months, differ a lot between
people of the same age, and fade unevenly when training stops. Muscle size is not a
promise this app makes.

# Gains are mostly neural

- A Canadian position paper concludes that strength gains in children come mainly from
  the nervous system, because strength rises far more than muscle size; more sensitive
  imaging suggests some growth in size may also occur.[^csep-2008]
- In a small trial with prepubertal children, 8 weeks of training raised arm strength by
  about 23 to 28% with no change in arm size, while the muscles' electrical activity rose
  by about 17%.[^ozmun-1994] That is children before puberty, younger than the keeper's
  band: it shows the mechanism, not the size of the gain to expect.
- Later in adolescence, hormones allow more muscle growth, and the ability to gain
  strength rises with age and maturity.[^behringer-2010] When that happens depends on each
  person's maturation, which varies widely among people of the same age.[^ioc-2015]
- **Rule for dossiers:** say "your body learns to use its muscles better" (coordination,
  recruitment, control). Never promise bigger, toned or shaped muscles, and say that
  growth and maturation timing differ from person to person. See the
  [claim rules](/dossiers/claim-rules.md).

# How big, and how fast

| Evidence | Who | Time frame | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta-analysis, 43 studies[^lesinski-2016] | Youth athletes, children to late teens | Weeks to months; programmes over about 23 weeks did best | Moderate effects on strength and jumping, small effects on sprint, agility and sport skills |
| Meta-analysis[^behringer-2010] | Children and adolescents | Longer studies did better | Strength gains grow with programme length and number of sets |
| Randomised trial[^granacher-2014] | School pupils in their early teens | 6 weeks, 2 sessions a week | Trunk strength tests 8 to 22% better; sideways jumping 4 to 5%; balance 2 to 3% |
| Trial[^ozmun-1994] | Prepubertal children | 8 weeks, 3 sessions a week | Arm strength about 23 to 28% better, no size change |

Two cautions for a dossier:

- The trial closest to the keeper's band compared two training groups and had no group
  that did not train, so part of the change may be learning the tests and normal
  growth.[^granacher-2014]
- The most effective doses in the athlete meta-analysis are heavy loads the app never
  prescribes.[^lesinski-2016][^training-rules] The app's bodyweight doses sit at the
  gentle end, so a dossier quotes the lower part of any range and says the gains build
  over months.

# When training stops

- In novice youth volleyball players in their early teens, jump gains after 12 weeks of
  neuromuscular training held through 8 weeks without it.[^nunes-2021] That is one
  study, for jumping; no study found here tracks trunk or hip gains after stopping in
  this band. A dossier says "keep it up about twice a week to keep the gains", not that
  gains last a set time.

# Injury: what the evidence allows

- The position statements say well-designed youth resistance training can improve
  resistance to sports injury, as part of a whole programme.[^lloyd-2014][^aap-2020]
- The best trial in youth football tested a whole warm-up programme (running, planks,
  strength, jumps, balance) over a season: overall injury risk was lower, but its main
  outcome, lower-limb injuries, did not reach significance.[^soligard-2008] No trial found
  here tests one bodyweight exercise on its own.
- **Rule for dossiers:** an exercise can be "part of the kind of warm-up and strength
  work linked to fewer injuries in youth football". It never "prevents injury" by itself.
