# Summary

Outdoor space adds two things a home lacks: room to sprint and a soft natural surface
for dives and jumps. Stairs add a no-cost conditioning option, with weaker evidence.
Heat is the main extra risk outdoors.

# A field or park

- **Room to sprint.** The sprint dose from a youth meta-analysis uses efforts of about
  20 m (about 22 yd) with long rests,[^moran-2017-sprint] which most homes cannot fit. A
  field or park makes the full sprint block possible.
- **A soft surface.** Grass lets the keeper practise dives and do jump work on a
  forgiving surface. The youth plyometric review judged jump training safe when safety
  guidelines are built in,[^johnson-2011] and a soft landing surface is one of the
  app's.
- **Goalkeeper-specific space.** A goal, or two markers as posts, lets the keeper work on
  positioning and diving across the goal; goalkeeper work is mostly short, explosive
  efforts in a confined area.[^white-2018]
- **Play counts too.** WHO counts all moderate-to-vigorous activity, including play and
  recreation, toward adolescents' daily activity.[^who-2020]
- Check the ground for holes, glass and slippery patches before each session.

# Stairs

- Short, hard stair-climbing bouts (for example three bouts of 20 seconds, three times a
  week for six weeks) improved aerobic fitness in previously untrained adult
  women.[^allison-2017] There is no comparable youth study in the sources found, so the
  app treats stair work as conditioning only, at moderate effort.
- **Rules:** climb up, walk down; use a handrail; dry stairs only; no jumping down stairs
  and no bounding up more than one step at a time.

# Heat and weather

- The IOC calls for education on exertional heat illness and a plan for responding to it
  in outdoor youth sport.[^ioc-2015]
- The app shortens or moves an outdoor session in hot weather, builds in water breaks,
  and lists heat-illness signs as a stop sign (see
  [training rules](/decisions/training-rules.md)).
